1. What Is an Exponent?

An exponent tells you how many times to multiply a base number by itself:

\[b^n = \underbrace{b \times b \times \cdots \times b}_{n \text{ times}}\]

For example:

Expression Calculation Result
\(2^1\) 2 2
\(2^2\) 2 × 2 4
\(2^3\) 2 × 2 × 2 8
\(2^{10}\) 2 × 2 × … 1024

Key rules of exponents

Rule Formula Example
Product rule \(b^m \times b^n = b^{m+n}\) \(2^3 \times 2^4 = 2^7 = 128\)
Quotient rule \(b^m / b^n = b^{m-n}\) \(2^5 / 2^2 = 2^3 = 8\)
Power of a power \((b^m)^n = b^{m \times n}\) \((2^3)^2 = 2^6 = 64\)
Zero exponent \(b^0 = 1\) \(5^0 = 1\)
Negative exponent \(b^{-n} = 1/b^n\) \(2^{-3} = 1/8 = 0.125\)
Fractional exponent \(b^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{b}\) \(9^{0.5} = 3\)

Try it in R

# Exponentiation in R uses ^
2^10          # 1024
[1] 1024
2^(-3)        # 0.125
[1] 0.125
9^(0.5)       # 3  (square root)
[1] 3
# The product rule: b^m * b^n = b^(m+n)
2^3 * 2^4     # 128
[1] 128
2^(3 + 4)     # 128 — same answer
[1] 128

2. What Is a Logarithm?

A logarithm is the inverse of exponentiation. It answers the question:

*“To what power must I raise the base* \(b\) to get \(x\)?”

\[\log_b(x) = n \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad b^n = x\]

Examples:

  • \(\log_2(8) = 3\) because \(2^3 = 8\)
  • \(\log_{10}(1000) = 3\) because \(10^3 = 1000\)

The natural logarithm

In ecology and population biology, we almost always use the natural logarithm, whose base is the mathematical constant \(e \approx 2.718\). It is written \(\ln(x)\) in mathematics and simply log(x) in R.

\[\ln(x) = \log_e(x)\]

Warning

R pitfall. log() in R is the natural logarithm (base \(e\)), not base 10. Use log10() for base-10 logs. In population dynamics, log() is almost always what you want.

Key rules of logarithms

Rule Formula Why it matters
Log of a product \(\ln(ab) = \ln(a) + \ln(b)\) Turns multiplication into addition
Log of a quotient \(\ln(a/b) = \ln(a) - \ln(b)\) Turns division into subtraction
Log of a power \(\ln(a^n) = n \cdot \ln(a)\) Brings exponents down as multipliers
Log of 1 \(\ln(1) = 0\)
Log of \(e\) \(\ln(e) = 1\) Definition of the natural log
Inverse property \(\ln(e^x) = x\) and \(e^{\ln(x)} = x\) log and exp cancel each other

Try it in R

log(1)            # 0
[1] 0
log(exp(1))       # 1  — log and exp cancel
[1] 1
exp(log(5))       # 5  — exp and log cancel
[1] 5
log10(1000)       # 3  — base-10 log
[1] 3
# Demonstrate the product rule
log(3 * 4)        # should equal log(3) + log(4)
[1] 2.484907
log(3) + log(4)   # same answer
[1] 2.484907
# Demonstrate the power rule
log(2^10)         # should equal 10 * log(2)
[1] 6.931472
10 * log(2)       # same answer
[1] 6.931472

3. The Number e

\(e \approx 2.71828\) is a fundamental mathematical constant. It appears whenever something grows or decays continuously at a constant proportional rate.

Where does e come from?

Imagine a bank account that earns 100% interest per year. If interest is compounded more and more frequently, the final balance after one year approaches a limit:

Compounding Formula Result
Once per year \((1 + 1)^1\) 2.000
Monthly \((1 + 1/12)^{12}\) 2.613
Daily \((1 + 1/365)^{365}\) 2.7146
Continuously \(\lim_{n \to \infty}(1 + 1/n)^n\) \(e = 2.71828\ldots\)

This limit defines \(e\). Populations reproduce more-or-less continuously rather than all at once, so \(e\) appears naturally in the equations you will encounter in Module 3.

# e in R
exp(1)          # 2.718282
[1] 2.718282
# Demonstrating the limit that defines e
n <- c(1, 12, 365, 1e6)
(1 + 1/n)^n     # approaches exp(1) as n increases
[1] 2.000000 2.613035 2.714567 2.718280

e as a base for exponents

The function \(e^x\) (written exp(x) in R) has a special property: its rate of change at any point equals its own value. This “self-referential” growth is exactly what happens when a population grows at a constant per-capita rate — which is why you’ll see \(e^{r_m t}\) in Module 3.

# exp(x) in R
exp(0)    # 1
[1] 1
exp(1)    # e ≈ 2.718
[1] 2.718282
exp(2)    # e^2 ≈ 7.389
[1] 7.389056
exp(-1)   # 1/e ≈ 0.368  (decay)
[1] 0.3678794

5. Self-Check Questions

Try these before starting Module 3.

Question 1. Simplify \(3^4 \times 3^{-2}\). What is the numerical answer?

Show answer

Using the product rule: \(3^4 \times 3^{-2} = 3^{4+(-2)} = 3^2 = \mathbf{9}\).

In R: 3^4 * 3^(-2) or 3^(4 + -2).

Question 2. What is \(\ln(e^5)\)? What rule did you use?

Show answer

By the inverse property, \(\ln(e^5) = \mathbf{5}\). The log and exponent cancel because they are inverse operations. In R: log(exp(5)).

Question 3. Use the power rule to simplify \(\ln(x^3)\).

Show answer

\(\ln(x^3) = 3 \cdot \ln(x)\). This is the log-of-a-power rule: the exponent comes down as a multiplier.

Question 4. Without using R, what is \(e^0\)?

Show answer

\(e^0 = \mathbf{1}\). Any number raised to the power zero equals 1 (the zero-exponent rule).

Summary

  • Exponents describe repeated multiplication: \(b^n\) multiplies \(b\) by itself \(n\) times.
  • Logarithms are the inverse of exponents: \(\ln(x)\) asks “to what power must I raise \(e\) to get \(x\)?”
  • The natural logarithm (base \(e\)) is the standard in ecology; in R it is log().
  • log() and exp() are inverses: they cancel each other.
  • The constant \(e \approx 2.718\) arises naturally from continuous compounding, making it the right base for continuous growth processes.
