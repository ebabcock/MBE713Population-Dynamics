Exponents & Logarithms for Population Dynamics

Learning objectives

  • Understand what logarithms mean and why they are the inverse of exponents.
  • Apply the natural logarithm and e to population growth equations.
  • Use R to compute exponentials and logarithms with confidence.

Module 3 introduces exponential population growth. The central equation, Nt = N0 ermt, involves both an exponent and a logarithm. If those feel unfamiliar, this short tutorial will give you the background you need.

1. What Is an Exponent?

An exponent tells you how many times to multiply a base number by itself:

2^1  =  2
2^2  =  2 × 2  =  4
2^3  =  2 × 2 × 2  =  8
2^10 =  1024

In general:  bn means "multiply b by itself n times."

Key rules of exponents

RuleFormulaExample
Product rulebm × bn = bm+n23 × 24 = 27 = 128
Quotient rulebm / bn = bm−n25 / 22 = 23 = 8
Power of a power(bm)n = bm×n(23)2 = 26 = 64
Zero exponentb0 = 150 = 1
Negative exponentb−n = 1/bn2−3 = 1/8 = 0.125
Fractional exponentb1/n = √nb90.5 = 3
Population dynamics link. In discrete-time growth, a population of 100 with λ = 1.25 after 5 years is:
N5 = 100 × 1.255 = 100 × 3.052 ≈ 305
The exponent is just the number of time steps.

Try it in R

# Exponentiation in R uses ^ or **
2^10          # 1024
1.25^5        # 3.051758
100 * 1.25^5  # 305.1758

2. What Is a Logarithm?

A logarithm is the inverse of exponentiation. It answers the question: "To what power must I raise b to get x?"

log base 2 of 8  =  3    because  2^3 = 8
log base 10 of 1000 = 3  because 10^3 = 1000

In ecology and population dynamics, we almost always use the natural logarithm, written ln or simply log in R. Its base is the mathematical constant e ≈ 2.718.

log(x) in R  =  ln(x)  =  log base e of x

Key rules of logarithms

RuleFormulaWhy it matters
Log of a productln(ab) = ln(a) + ln(b)Turns multiplication into addition
Log of a quotientln(a/b) = ln(a) − ln(b)Turns division into subtraction
Log of a powerln(an) = n × ln(a)Brings exponents down as multipliers
Log of 1ln(1) = 0No growth means zero log-growth rate
Log of eln(e) = 1Definition of natural log
Inverse of expln(ex) = x   and   eln(x) = xLog and exp cancel each other
Common pitfall. log() in R is the natural logarithm (base e), not log base 10. Use log10() for base-10 and log2() for base-2. In most population dynamics work, log() is what you want.

Try it in R

log(1)          # 0
log(exp(1))     # 1   (exp is e^x)
exp(log(5))     # 5   (they cancel)
log(8) / log(2) # 3   (log base 2 of 8, using change-of-base)

# Demonstrate the product rule
log(3 * 4)      # should equal log(3) + log(4)
log(3) + log(4) # same answer

3. The Number e and Why It Appears in Population Growth

e ≈ 2.71828 is a special mathematical constant. It arises naturally whenever something grows (or decays) continuously at a constant proportional rate.

Imagine a bank account earning 100% interest per year. If interest is compounded:

  • Once per year: $1 → $2.000
  • Monthly: $1 → $2.613
  • Daily: $1 → $2.7146
  • Continuously: $1 → $e = $2.71828…

Populations reproduce more-or-less continuously, not just at New Year's. So the natural model for continuous population growth is:

Nt = N0 erm t

where rm is the instantaneous (continuous) growth rate per unit time.

Connecting discrete and continuous time. In Module 3 you will see both:
  • Discrete:   Nt+1 = Nt × λ
  • Continuous: Nt = N0 erm t
They are related by:   rm = ln(λ)   and   λ = erm.
You will use these conversions throughout the course. 
lambda <- 1.25
rm     <- log(lambda)   # continuous rate from discrete lambda
rm                       # 0.2231

exp(rm)                  # back to lambda: 1.25

# Both formulas give the same N at integer time steps
N0 <- 100
t  <- 5
N0 * lambda^t            # discrete:    305.18
N0 * exp(rm * t)         # continuous:  305.18

4. Why Logs Make Exponential Growth Look Linear

Start with the continuous growth equation and take the natural log of both sides:

N_t  = N0 * exp(rm * t)
ln(N_t)  = ln(N0 * exp(rm * t))
ln(N_t)  = ln(N0) + ln(exp(rm * t))   [product rule]
ln(N_t)  = ln(N0) + rm * t            [inverse rule]

This is the equation of a straight line:   y = intercept + slope × x,   where:

  • y = ln(Nt)
  • intercept = ln(N0)
  • slope = rm
  • x = t

This is exactly why, in Module 3, we fit a linear regression to log(population) against time to estimate rm from data.

# Simulate exponential growth and recover rm with a linear regression
N0 <- 50
rm <- 0.15
t  <- 0:20
N  <- N0 * exp(rm * t)

fit <- lm(log(N) ~ t)
coef(fit)
# (Intercept)          t
#   3.912023     0.150000   <-- intercept = ln(50), slope = rm = 0.15

6. Self-Check Questions

Try these before starting Module 3. Answers are in the drop-down below each question.

  1. If λ = 1.10, what is rm? What does the sign of rm tell you?

    Show answer

    log(1.10) ≈ 0.0953 per year. A positive value means the population is growing.

  2. A population has rm = −0.05 per year. What is λ? Is the population growing or declining?

    Show answer

    exp(−0.05) ≈ 0.951. Because λ < 1, the population is declining.

  3. Starting from N0 = 200 and rm = 0.08 per year, what is the population after 10 years?

    Show answer

    200 * exp(0.08 * 10) = 200 × e0.8 ≈ 445.

  4. Why does plotting log(N) vs. time turn an exponential curve into a straight line?

    Show answer

    Taking the log of Nt = N0 ermt gives ln(Nt) = ln(N0) + rm × t, which is a linear equation in t. The slope is rm.

Summary

  • Exponents describe repeated multiplication. In population dynamics, λt gives discrete growth over t time steps.
  • Logarithms are the inverse of exponents. The natural log (base e) is the standard in ecology.
  • The constant e arises from continuous compounding, making it the natural base for continuous population growth.
  • Converting between discrete (λ) and continuous (rm) rates:   rm = ln(λ)   and   λ = erm.
  • A log-scale plot of exponential growth is a straight line, and the slope equals rm — a fact used directly in Module 3's regression analysis.
